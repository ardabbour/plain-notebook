There’s something good about a page that just lets you read it.

It arrives quickly. The words are large enough. The links look like links. Nothing asks for your email before you’ve decided whether you want to stay.

## Start with the words

A personal website doesn’t need to make an entrance. It can open with a thought, a useful explanation, or a simple introduction.

The structure should help the words do their job: headings that tell you where you are, short paragraphs, and enough space to breathe.

## Make wandering easy

A notebook has a different rhythm from a feed. There’s no pressure to read everything in order. A page can point sideways to another idea, or back to an older one.

Ordinary links do this beautifully. They also work with a keyboard, can be saved for later, and still make sense when the styling disappears.

## Keep what earns its place

Simplicity doesn’t mean removing every convenience. A table of contents helps with a long page. A dark theme helps a reader settle in. Another language opens the door to someone else.

The question is small: does this help someone publish, find, or read a page?

> Make a place worth visiting. Let the reader decide how long to stay.

## Leave it unfinished

Publish the useful part. Correct it when you learn more. Let the site grow at the pace of your attention.

That feels like a good enough place to begin.
